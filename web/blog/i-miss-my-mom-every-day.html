I Miss My Mom Every Day. That Day Writes Me a Story About Her Every Morning.

July 25, 2026 · 4 min read

My mom passed away in 2020. I thought I had all her photos in one folder. I didn't.

Last month, I installed That Day. It scanned every hard drive I own. It found photos of my mom across ten years, five devices, three cities. Some I'd never seen. Some I'd forgotten completely.

Here's what happened next.

July 14 — A Tuesday I Didn't Remember

The first morning, a story arrived in my inbox. It was July 14 — a random Tuesday in 2019. I'd never have chosen this date myself. I don't remember that day. But That Day found a photo of my mom and me making noodles in her kitchen. She was laughing at something.

The story That Day wrote was short — maybe 150 words. It mentioned the afternoon light, the steam rising from the pot, her hands rolling dough. It didn't need to invent anything. It had the photo. I had annotated three words: "Mom, kitchen, making noodles." The AI did the rest.

I forwarded the email to my sister. She cried.

I Didn't Realize I Was Grieving Wrong

After my mom passed, I stopped looking at old photos. Every time I opened an album, I'd scan for her face. When I found it, I'd close the window. It hurt too much.

That Day doesn't ask me to go looking. It picks a date across every year — including the years she was in them — and shows me what happened on that date. It's curated grief, in a way. A small, deliberate dose.

And because the AI writes the story and the voice reads it aloud, I don't have to sit in silence with the memory. Someone is telling it to me.

The Photos I Almost Lost

That Day found photos of my mom from:

  • 2012 — A birthday dinner I'd completely blocked out
  • 2015 — A weekend at a lake house I swore never happened
  • 2018 — Her tending her garden, one of her only peaceful hobbies
  • 2019 — That noodle-making day

I had all of these on my computer. I just never knew they were there. They lived inside folders called Backup_2015 and 2019-07 misc — the digital equivalent of a shoebox under the bed.

That Day found them by matching the date. It didn't need album names or tags. Just the EXIF timestamp and my computer's clock.

What I Do Every Morning Now

I wake up, grab my phone, and check my email. The subject line says "Your day in stories — June 8, 2016" or "March 22, 2019" or any other date. I open it. I read it. Sometimes I tap the play button and let the voice read to me while I make coffee.

The email goes to a folder I created called "Her." I've been saving the PDFs. 24 days so far. 24 entries in a journal I didn't have to write.

It's Not About the AI

People ask me: "Doesn't it feel fake, having a computer write about your mom?"

No. The AI writes the sentences. But the memory — that's mine. I supplied the photo. I supplied the three words. The computer just connects the dots. The story is still about her.

And the photo is real. That's what matters. The AI is just the hand that turns the page.
